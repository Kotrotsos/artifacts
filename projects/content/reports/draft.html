The Tone Fix That Broke the Agent

A support team changed one line to make their AI sound warmer. Three weeks later, one in eight replies was friendly, fluent, and missing the actual answer, and nobody had noticed. The fix was not a better prompt. It was the thing almost every agent team skips: evaluation.

Here is a story I have watched play out more than once. The details below are one version of it, but the shape is always the same.

A mid-market company built a customer support agent. It read an incoming email, looked up the order in their system, and drafted a reply for a human to approve. The demo was flawless. Someone typed in a tricky refund question, the agent pulled the right order, wrote a clear and accurate response, and the room nodded. They shipped it behind a human approval step. For a month it looked good, approvals got faster and lazier, and most replies started going out close to untouched.

Then a product manager got a complaint that the replies sounded robotic. Reasonable. She edited the system prompt to make the agent warmer and more human, tested it on a few tickets, saw friendlier replies, and shipped the change.

Three weeks later the complaints started. Roughly one reply in eight was warm, apologetic, perfectly pleasant, and contained no actual resolution. No order number, no refund amount, no next step. Just empathy. The tone edit had quietly changed how the agent prioritized, and on certain ticket shapes it now led with warmth and dropped the specifics entirely. Every one of those replies looked right. That is exactly why nobody caught it until customers did.

Three things to take away:

  • The instinct that made software reliable for decades, run it and see if it looks right, is the precise failure mode of agents. A confidently wrong answer looks right. A friendly non-answer looks right.
  • The fix is not a smarter prompt. It is a small suite of checks that grade the agent's output against a definition of good, run on every change, so a regression shows up in seconds instead of three weeks.
  • Those checks are the actual engineering of an agent. Everything else, the prompt, the model, the orchestration, is a hypothesis. The evals are what turn a guess into a measured decision.

Why "test it and see" stopped working

For decades software ran on a comfortable assumption. The same input gives the same output. Pass two and three to your add function, get five, every time. Unit tests work because behavior is deterministic. You ship on Friday because a green suite today means a green suite tomorrow unless someone changed the code.

Build on a language model and that floor moves. Run the same prompt twice and you get two different replies, and both of them might be fine. Ask the support agent to handle a refund and there is no single correct string. There are thousands of good replies and millions of bad ones, and the line between them is not something you can check with string matching. A unit test asks whether the output equals an exact value. For an agent that question is close to meaningless.

So teams fall back on the only thing left, which is reading a few outputs and going on the feeling that they look right. That feeling is the trap. The support team had been running on it for a month without knowing, because looking right is the one thing a fluent model is always good at, even when it is wrong.

What they actually built

After the incident, the team did the thing they should have done before launch. They built an eval harness, and it was less work than the incident cost them.

They pulled sixty real tickets from the last quarter, the messy ones included, and for each they wrote down what a good resolution actually contained. That golden set was the whole foundation. Then they wrote four checks, one concern each, because a single grader that judges everything at once is impossible to calibrate and useless when it fails.

The first check was pure code and cost nothing to run. Does the reply contain an order reference and a concrete next step. That one check, run against the warmer prompt, would have caught the entire incident. The format pass rate dropped from ninety-nine percent to eighty-six the moment the tone edit landed, and a number moving that far is impossible to miss in a way that a friendly reply is not.

The second check used a second model as a grader, asking whether every factual claim in the reply was grounded in the order data the agent retrieved, with no invented dates or amounts. The third enforced policy: never promise a refund above the amount that needs human approval, never commit to a delivery date that is not in the system. The fourth, also a model grader, judged whether the tone was warm and on brand, the very thing the prompt edit was trying to improve. Now warmth was a measured dimension too, so improving it could no longer silently break something else.

None of this is exotic. A golden set, a few graders, one concern each. The hard part was not technical. It was deciding to measure instead of trusting the demo.

Evals are speed, not a seatbelt

It is tempting to file all of this under safety, a cost you pay to avoid embarrassment. That undersells it. A strong test suite does not make a codebase fast because it prevents bugs. It makes it fast because it gives you permission to change things aggressively. You can rewrite a module on a hunch, run the suite, and know in seconds whether you helped or hurt.

The same thing happened to the support team, and this is the part that changed how they worked. A month after building the harness, a new model came out that looked sharper in every quick test they tried. The old instinct would have been to swap it in and feel good about it. Instead they ran the suite. Faithfulness dropped six points, almost entirely on refund tickets, the highest-stakes category they had. They held the upgrade, adjusted the prompt, and shipped the new model a week later when the number recovered. Without the suite they would have shipped a quiet regression into their most sensitive flow and found out from customers again.

A change with a number attached is a decision. A change without one is a gamble. The difference between a team that fears every change and a team that ships improvements weekly is not talent. It is whether the change comes back with a number.

There is a compounding payoff. Every failure they found in production became a permanent test case. The friendly-non-answer tickets went straight into the golden set, so that specific failure can never silently return. Over a year the suite becomes a record of every way the agent has ever gone wrong and a guarantee it will not go wrong that way again without someone knowing. A competitor cannot copy that, because they do not have your traffic and have not seen your failures. The eval suite stops being overhead and becomes the one asset that is genuinely yours.

What to measure, and what not to

The instinct to build one giant check that tests everything is strong and wrong. Split evaluation into single concerns, so that when something fails you know exactly which one.

One distinction is worth holding onto. A capability eval is a hill you are climbing, something the agent is currently bad at, where you iterate until it passes. The moment it passes you promote it to a regression eval, a permanent check whose only job is to make sure the agent never loses a skill it already had. A healthy suite is a steady conversion of one into the other, climbing a new hill while protecting every hill you already climbed.

And one warning that saves you grief. Do not write checks that dictate how the agent works, asserting it must call this tool, then that one, then decide this. A smarter model will often solve in three steps what used to take six, and if your check grades the route instead of the destination, every genuine improvement breaks your tests for no reason. Grade what the agent produced, not the path it took to get there.

From demoing to measuring

The hardest part of all this is cultural. Teams that build agents grow up on the demo, because the demo is how you get buy-in and feel progress. The demo is also the enemy of reliability, because it shows you one run that worked while production is decided by the thousand runs nobody watched. The support team had a beautiful demo. It told them nothing about the one-in-eight case that eventually called them.

You will know a team has made the shift when the question after a change stops being "does it look better" and becomes "what did the score do." You will know an individual has made it when their first instinct on a failure is not to tweak the prompt but to read the trace and ask why. Fifteen minutes reading real outputs is worth more than hours of fiddling with wording, because fiddling without reading is just a more elaborate form of guessing.

Everything you do to an agent, the prompt, the tools, the model, the orchestration, is a hypothesis that the change made it better. Without evals, your guesses stay guesses and your users run the experiment for you, which is the slowest and most expensive feedback loop there is. With evals, every guess comes back with a number and the agent improves on a measured gradient instead of a hopeful one. That is the engineering. The prompt is the easy part. The measurement layer underneath is what turns a demo you are proud of into a system you can change without fear and trust enough to put your name on.
