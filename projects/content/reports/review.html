Model Review

Claude Fable 5, reviewed

Anthropic just put a Mythos-class model in public hands. The benchmarks are real and the autonomy is genuinely new. The catch is that it does not know when to stop, and that shows up on your invoice.

Released June 9, 2026 Price $10 / $50 per million tokens Context 1M in, 128K out Verdict Frontier, for the right job

Three things to take away

1

Fable 5 is the strongest generally available model on nearly every coding, knowledge-work, and computer-use benchmark, and the gap is largest on long, messy, multi-step tasks rather than single prompts. This is not a one or two point release. On SWE-bench Pro it clears the field by 11 points.

2

Its defining trait is autonomy: it keeps working where older models stop to ask. That is the product. It is also the problem, because it runs until something cuts it off, which makes it slow and roughly twice the cost of Opus 4.8. Judge it by cost per solved task, not by token price.

3

Use it where a job is genuinely long and worth a thorough result: multi-day migrations, deep research, agent harnesses with clear stop conditions. For quick edits, high-throughput review, or anything latency-sensitive, Opus 4.8 is still the smarter default.

What it actually is

Anthropic ships its frontier capability in two forms. Mythos 5 is the unrestricted model, available to vetted users with cyber safeguards lifted. Fable 5 is the same underlying model with guardrails on, released to everyone on Pro, Max, Team, and Enterprise plans. Both sit in a new tier the company calls Mythos-class, which it positions a step above the Opus line.

The pitch is not "smarter answers." It is endurance. Fable 5 is built to run for days, planning across stages, spawning sub-agents, and checking its own work, with a 1 million token context and a new file-based memory it uses to write notes mid-task and refer back to them. The headline customer proof point: Stripe reports it completed a migration across a 50 million line Ruby codebase in a single day, work a team estimated at more than two months by hand.

Two non-coding results stand out. On GDPval-AA, a benchmark of real-world economic knowledge-work tasks, Fable 5 scores 1932 and takes the top spot, well ahead of Gemini 3.1 Pro at 1314. And it is the first model to break 90% on a core long-running analytics benchmark, a 10 point jump over Opus. Artificial Analysis put it at number one on its Intelligence Index, roughly 5 points clear of any other lab's best model. The pattern is consistent: the longer and more tool-heavy the task, the wider Fable's lead.

Real-world use

Benchmarks rhyme with the hands-on reports, which is not always true. The clearest independent read comes from Simon Willison, who spent about five and a half hours putting it through real work.

Fable did not just solve the feature he asked for in his Datasette Agent project. It also identified and then fixed four separate issues in his underlying LLM library, unprompted. He called the model "something of a beast" and said the harder challenge was "finding tasks that it can't do."
Simon Willison, hands-on impressions, June 9, 2026

That captures the real shift. The standout is not raw IQ on a single answer, it is how much ground it covers without being told. Zapier, which named it the new leader on its AutomationBench, put the same trait more bluntly: "Where Opus stops to ask, Fable 5 keeps looking." Notion describes turning a pile of messy notes into a working project plan in one pass. Hebbia reports its highest-ever score on a finance benchmark, and the trading firm IMC says it cleared their analysis evaluations almost across the board.

Vision is part of this, not a side feature. Fable 5 reads charts, tables, diagrams, and PDFs, and it uses sight to check its own coding work against an original design. In one independent test it rebuilt a Netflix page from a screenshot and turned a hand-drawn wireframe into a working React dashboard, filling in missing colors, spacing, and interaction with sensible defaults. The reviewer's honest caveat is the recurring one: some of the output "leaned toward extra styling that might need refinement for production." It gets you most of the way, not all of it.

Where real-world use breaks down

The autonomy that makes Fable impressive is also what makes it expensive and occasionally maddening. It tends to run until something stops it. Willison spent $110.42 in a single day of normal use. The model is slow next to Opus, and in an agent loop without firm stop rules it will happily keep going long after a cheaper model would have called it done. None of this is a dealbreaker. It is a planning constraint. You set the budget and the stop condition, or the model sets them for you.

Coding

This is where Fable 5 is most obviously ahead, and also where the tradeoffs are sharpest. The benchmark wins are real: 95% on SWE-bench Verified, an 11 point lead on SWE-bench Pro, and a FrontierCode Diamond score more than double Opus on the hardest set. Cursor calls it state of the art on its internal CursorBench and says it "opened up a class of long-horizon problems that were out of reach." GitHub shipped it into Copilot on day one, citing autonomy and reliability that "exceeded previous benchmarks." Cognition reports it as the highest-scoring model on FrontierCode even at medium effort.

The most useful independent coding read is from CodeRabbit, because it is not a fan letter. They ran it two ways, as an autonomous builder and as a code reviewer, and got opposite results.

Strong at: building

  • From vague prompts it ships complete projects, not prototype shells
  • Explores the environment and its tools before writing code
  • Real architecture across files: proper layering, state, production builds
  • Finds non-obvious solution paths; less narrating, more doing

Weak at: stopping and reviewing

  • 19 of 33 coding tasks ran to timeout rather than converging
  • As a reviewer, lower precision than Opus 4.8 (32.8% vs 35.5%)
  • More nitpicky, higher comment volume, weaker on the hardest cases
  • Heavy output token use makes high-throughput workflows costly

CodeRabbit's conclusion is the practical one: keep Opus 4.8 as your default code reviewer, and reach for Fable 5 on autonomous build projects that have a clear time budget and an explicit stop condition. That maps exactly to the cost story. Fable is not the model you fire at a one-line fix. It is the model you point at a migration, a greenfield feature, or a multi-file refactor and let run, with guardrails, because the thorough result is worth the longer, pricier session.

How to actually deploy it for code

  • Give it a stop condition. A turn cap, a budget ceiling, a "done when tests pass" check. Left open, it will keep going.
  • Pay for caching. The 90% input-token discount on cached prompts materially changes the math on long agent runs.
  • Default to Opus 4.8 for the small stuff. Quick edits, reviews, and latency-sensitive work do not need Mythos-class endurance and should not pay for it.
  • Measure cost per solved task, not the per-token rate. On the hard, long jobs Fable wins on that metric even at double the price. On easy jobs it loses badly.

The price is double Opus across the board, and because Fable produces a lot of output and runs long, the effective bill climbs faster than the rate card suggests. That is the single most important thing to internalize before you turn it loose. The flip side is that for genuinely hard, long-horizon work it can collapse weeks into a day, and on cost per finished outcome that is a bargain. Both things are true. Which one you experience depends entirely on whether you point it at work that needs it.

Safety, briefly

Because the underlying model is Mythos-class, Fable 5 ships with a two-stage classifier that reroutes prompts touching cybersecurity, biology, chemistry, and health to Opus 4.8 instead. Anthropic says this catches fewer than 5% of sessions, and rerouted requests are not billed at Fable rates. A 30-day data retention requirement applies for safety monitoring, which is worth knowing if you operate under strict data-handling rules.

Who it is for

Reach for Fable 5

Multi-day migrations, deep research and analysis, agent harnesses, greenfield builds, document-heavy finance and science work, anything where autonomy and thoroughness are the point and a longer session is acceptable.

Stay on Opus 4.8

Quick edits, high-throughput code review, latency-sensitive or interactive use, cost-sensitive pipelines, and any task where "good enough, now, cheap" beats "exhaustive, eventually, pricey."

Bottom line

Fable 5 is the most capable model the public can use right now, and on the long, ambiguous, tool-heavy work that looks like an actual job rather than a quiz, the lead is not subtle. The honest reservation is not about capability, it is about discipline. The model does not know when it is finished, so you have to tell it, with budgets and stop conditions, or the bill writes the review for you. Treat it as a specialist you bring in for the hard, long jobs, give it guardrails, and it earns its rate. Aim it at everything and it will quietly drain your account proving it could have.
