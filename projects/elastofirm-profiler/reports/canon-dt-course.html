0The problem: rotation destroys the descriptor

The current matcher describes a profile with one whole-image HOG vector (a histogram of edge-gradient directions over the whole picture). That vector is fast and works on clean scans, but it is tied to the orientation of the page. Rotate the same profile by 25° and the gradients all point in new directions, so the vector barely resembles the original.

→

To a human these are obviously the same part. To whole-image HOG they are nearly strangers:

current matcher similarity 54% canon_dt similarity 98%

The idea. If we always rotate every profile to the same canonical pose before describing it, rotation stops mattering. That is what canon does. Then we swap the raw stroke for its distance transform (the dt) so the descriptor reads the shape's body, not the exact line. Together: canon_dt.

1Image moments: finding the shape's own axis

To straighten a shape we first need to know which way it is pointing. We get that from image moments, the statistical mass distribution of the black pixels, exactly like the centre of mass and the axis of inertia of a physical object.

The centroid is the average pixel position. The second moments describe how the mass is spread around it: mu20 (spread left-right), mu02 (up-down), mu11 (diagonal tilt). The direction the shape is most stretched along, its principal axis, falls straight out of them:

theta = 0.5 · atan2( 2·mu11 , mu20 − mu02 )

Here the principal axis comes out at about -26° from horizontal. The orange ellipse is the shape's "footprint of inertia"; its long direction is the axis we will rotate onto the horizontal. Crucially this is computed the same way at index time and at match time, so it is a property of the shape, not of the page.

Rotating the axis flat removes the angle, but a horizontal axis still leaves four possible poses (the shape could be flipped left-right or top-bottom). We pin down a single one using the third-order moments, which measure skew, i.e. which end is heavier:

mu30 < 0 means the heavy end points left, so we turn it 180°. Then mu03 < 0 means the heavy end points up, so we flip it vertically. After these two deterministic tests the hook-foot always ends up in the same corner, for every copy of the shape.

Why deterministic flips matter. If we left the pose to chance, two scans of the same part could canonicalize into mirror images and score as different. The moment-sign rules guarantee the same shape always lands the same way up.

4The distance transform: describe the body, not the line

Even straightened, raw HOG over a thin stroke is twitchy: it keys on the two parallel edges of the line, so a slightly thicker or thinner draw of the same profile shifts the descriptor. The distance transform fixes this by replacing every foreground pixel with its distance to the nearest background pixel. A thin line becomes a low ridge, a thick body a tall plateau, a smooth field of "how deep inside the shape am I".

We then run HOG over that depth field instead of the raw image. Now the descriptor reads the shape's medial structure, the skeleton and how fat it is along its length, which is far steadier against line-weight and tiny perturbations, and it gives the gradients real interior content to grip instead of mostly-empty white space.

6The failure mode: when the shape is square-ish

Everything so far rests on one assumption: that the shape has a clear long axis. For an elongated extrusion it does. But canon_dt has a real Achilles heel, and it is worth seeing honestly. The principal axis is only as trustworthy as the gap between the two moment eigenvalues (λmax vs λmin, the long and short spread). Their ratio is the shape's elongation. As it approaches 1, the shape becomes rotationally symmetric and the axis stops meaning anything.

Look at the inertia ellipse above: it is almost a circle. With mu20 ≈ mu02 and mu11 ≈ 0, the axis formula 0.5·atan2(2·mu11, mu20−mu02) is dividing by nearly zero, so a sliver of scan noise swings the chosen angle wildly. A true square has 90° symmetry, which the 180°/mirror flip rules do not even model. The result: the same part, scanned at different angles, canonicalizes to different poses.

How bad, and for how much of the catalogue?

We measured rotation recovery (rotate a profile, search it back in the 4k catalogue) split by elongation. The failure is a sharp cliff, not a gentle slope:

canon_dt is solid from ratio 1.05 upward (94–99%). It only breaks below 1.05, and in this catalogue only 4.8% of profiles sit there, the genuinely square / round cross-sections (square tube, round bar). So the weakness is real but narrow.

The fix: an eccentricity gate. Stamp each profile with its elongation ratio at index time. For the ~95% above 1.05, use canon_dt. For the ~5% below, do not canonicalize, route them to a rotation-invariant path instead: ORB + RANSAC (rotation-proof by construction), or a multi-orientation sweep (0/90/180/270) for near-square parts. The gate is a small safety valve, not a redesign.

7What it fixes, and what it does not

On the full 12k catalogue, taking real profiles, rotating them, and searching them back, the difference is stark:

casecurrent (global HOG)canon_dt
profile rotated ~20°often lost (rank in the hundreds)found #1–2 at 97–99%
tight vs loose margin22% top-1~100%
scanner noise50%82%
It is not a similarity-of-gestalt fix. canon_dt removes pose differences (rotation, flip, margin, scale). It does not make two profiles that merely share a motif but have different proportions score as the same part: those correctly stay around 50%, because they really are different shapes in the same pose.

And strong invariance has a price: occasionally canonicalizing makes a shape generic enough that unrelated profiles crowd in around it. That is why production keeps an ORB + RANSAC geometric rescore on top of the canon_dt candidate list, to re-verify that the actual keypoint geometry lines up and break ties a single global vector cannot.
