Spaces and Sharing

How Temple lets people keep private work, contribute to shared datasources, and share specific things with specific people, without personal data leaking into the shared world or project data accidentally landing in a personal store.

The two axes

Every safety property comes from keeping two ideas strictly apart. Most cross-contamination bugs are really one of these two being confused for the other.

Ownership immutable

Every entity belongs to exactly one home space, set when it is created and never changed by a normal write. The home space decides where writes land, the foreign-key boundary, and provenance.

Access revocable

Who may read, comment on, or write a thing. Grants are layered on top of ownership. They are audited, reversible, and they never move data between spaces.

The key idea Sharing with a person or a group is purely the access axis. A personal note you share with a colleague stays in your personal space; they simply hold a read grant on it. Nothing moves.

Spaces

A space is the home, and the owner, of the entities inside it. There are three kinds, all living inside an org. Org isolation is the outer hard wall; spaces partition within the org.

KindHome forMembers
Personalone user's private datathe owner only (implicit)
Teama named group's shared dataexplicit members in space_members
Companyorg-wide shared dataevery org member (implicit)
Share, or use a space? Share when access is the point and you stay the owner. Use a team or company space when joint ownership is the point. If you keep sharing the same personal collection with the same five people, that is the signal to promote it into a team space.

Ownership and write resolution

The dangerous moment is a write: where does the new data land? Temple resolves the home space of a new entity in a fixed order, so the answer is never a silent guess.

  1. an explicit space argument, if one was given;
  2. else the parent or collection's home space, so contributing into a shared collection lands in that collection, not in the writer's own store;
  3. else the actor's personal space, which is the safe default.

A write to an existing entity always resolves to that entity's home space, never the writer's. Editing a shared document edits it in place rather than forking a private copy. This is exactly what answers the two original worries: you can contribute to something shared with you without it secretly landing in your own store, and your edits do not silently leave the shared space.

Moving an entity from one space to another is a separate, audited transfer (promote) operation. It is the only thing that crosses a boundary, and it is deliberate, never a side effect.

Sharing and grants

A grant says: principal P may act at level L on target T. That is the whole vocabulary of sharing.

Principals (who you can share with)

KindMeaning
usera single person, matched by email
groupan ad-hoc named set of users, like "launch reviewers"
spacea team space, whose membership doubles as a principal

Targets and levels

You can grant on a single entity, a collection (a container whose members inherit the grant), or a whole space. Levels are ordered: read < comment < write.

Why sharing is the safe verb A grant never moves data, so it cannot leak by accident. It is reversible: revoke and the access is gone, with the whole history kept. Re-granting the same pair revokes the old grant and writes a new one, so there is at most one live grant per pair and every change is auditable.

Safe defaults and asymmetry

The two failure modes are not equally bad, so they are not guarded equally.

DirectionWhat it isGuard
Personal to sharedconfidentiality breach: visible to others, hard to retractdefault-deny; explicit confirmation
Shared to personalgovernance sprawl: annoying, recoverablelow-friction; default-allow
  • Writes to a shared space require an explicit membership grant; the bouncer rejects them otherwise.
  • Writes to your personal space are default-allow.
  • Granting read is low-friction. Granting write, or sharing anything out of a space you do not personally own, is echoed back and confirmed before it commits.
  • If a write scope is ever ambiguous, it defaults to personal. The failure mode becomes "my data stayed private," never "my data went public."

Cross-space foreign keys

Temple's relations are typed foreign keys, checked statically by the verifier and at runtime by the bouncer. The space rule extends that check.

  • A relation may not point across a space boundary unless the blueprint declares it cross-space.
  • A cross-space reference, when allowed, requires the actor to be able to read both sides.
  • Linked records are never auto-shared. Sharing one record does not transitively expose the records it points at.

This stops a personal note from hard-linking a shared record, which would break for everyone else, and stops a shared record from depending on something only you can see.

Bouncer evaluation

resolveAccess(actor, entity, need) is the entry point. It is an AND of two gates: the RBAC capability (can this role read or write at all) and the access decision (can this actor touch this specific entity).

// 1. Hard walls
if (entity.org_id !== actor.orgId) deny("cross-org")

// 2. RBAC capability gate (role-wide)
need === "read" ? require can(actor, "entity.read")
                  : require can(actor, "entity.update")

// 3. Access gate: most permissive of ownership OR grants
evaluateAccess(need, {
  ownership:  owner of personal home  -> write
              member of team home      -> write
              any member, company home -> write
  grants:     strongest live grant whose principal matches the actor
              (their user id, their groups, their team spaces)
              and whose target matches the entity, its collection,
              or its home space
})

// satisfied when rank(level) >= rank(need),  read=1 comment=2 write=3

Ownership and grants are evaluated together and the most permissive wins for the access axis; the RBAC capability is an AND gate on top, so a viewer can never write, however widely a thing is shared. The pure decision lives in src/auth/access.ts and is unit-testable without a database.

Tools

ToolWhat it does
grant_accessShare an entity or collection with a user, group, or team. Read is low-friction; write or sharing out of a space you do not own is confirmed first.

list_grantsWho an entity is shared with: every live grant, level, granter, expiry.

grant_access

grant_access({
  entityId:   string,           // the entity or container to share
  principal:  string,           // user email | group name | team name
  level:      "read" | "comment" | "write",
  targetKind: "entity" | "collection",  // optional, default entity
  expiresAt:  string             // optional ISO timestamp; auto-expires
})

Only the home-space owner (personal), a team-space manager, or an org admin or owner may grant access on a target.

Schema

Defined in sql/003_spaces_grants.sql. Five new tables plus one column.

spaces(id, org_id, kind, name, owner_user_id, archived)
space_members(id, space_id, user_id, role)        // member | manager
groups(id, org_id, name, created_by)
group_members(id, group_id, user_id)
grants(id, org_id,
       principal_kind, principal_id,                // user | group | space
       target_kind, target_id,                      // entity | collection | space
       level, granted_by, granted_at,
       expires_at, revoked_at, revoked_by)
entities.space_id                                  // new, immutable home space

The live-grant uniqueness per (principal, target) pair is enforced in the app layer, by revoking the prior grant before inserting, so the schema stays portable across PostgreSQL and pg-mem.

Prompting guide

The habit worth building: name the destination only when it is shared. "Personal" is silence; "shared," "team," and "company" are spoken words. The dangerous direction always requires an affirmative act of typing.

Write to personal (the common case)
Jot down a note: revisit the bouncer FK rules next week.
Lands in your personal space by default. No ceremony.
Write to shared (must be explicit)
Add this to the company datasource: Q3 onboarding checklist, owner is Ops.
The word "company" is the trigger. The agent echoes "writing to the shared store, visible org-wide, confirm?" before committing.
Ambiguous on purpose
Save the new client contact for Acme.
The agent asks "personal contacts or the shared CRM?" rather than guessing. If it ever silently picks shared, the guardrails are too weak.
Share with a person
Share my Project X collection with alice@acme.com, read-only.
A read grant. Project X stays in your personal space; Alice just gets access.
Share with a group
Give the design group write access to the roadmap.
A write grant to a group principal. The agent confirms first because it is write-level.
Time-boxed
Share this record with bob@acme.com until Friday.
Sets expiresAt; the grant auto-expires, no cleanup needed.
Revoke
Stop sharing the pricing draft with Alice.
Instant and audited. Revocation never needs confirmation.
Audit what is exposed
Who can see this? / What have I shared out of my personal store?
show_access renders the sharing panel; the grant ledger is the audit you actually want.
Use shared as reference, keep output private
Using the shared roadmap as reference, draft my own private planning notes.
Reads shared, writes personal. The cross-space FK rule keeps the personal note from hard-linking a shared record; it references by id only.
Promote (the only bridge across a boundary)
Take my personal draft of the pricing page and promote it to the team space.
The explicit transfer op. Shows a diff and confirms, because it crosses the confidentiality boundary.
